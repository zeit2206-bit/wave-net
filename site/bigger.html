Why is the standard network so fast?
  • The job is small: ~118,000 multiply-adds per image; the CPU does hundreds of billions per second.
  • The CPU is built for it: one instruction multiplies and adds 8 numbers at once, on 16 threads.
  • 40 years of tuning: the library keeps data in the CPU's small fast memory (cache) and reads it in order.

Picture a well-organized factory: every worker has their parts at hand. Our gates were workers walking across the warehouse for each part (random wiring = scattered memory reads).

Part 1 · A bigger network (keeping the laptop's limits in mind)
  • All 60,000 training digits instead of 20,000.
  • 4 brightness questions per pixel instead of 2 (3,136 bits per image).
  • 6 layers × 12,000 gates, each starting as "pass-through" (helps deep circuits train).
  • About 1 GB of memory; training took ~49 minutes.

For fairness, the standard network was also retrained on all 60,000 digits: it went from 95.3% to 97.3%.

Surprise: 50,070 of the 72,000 gates (70%) ended up as "pass-through": they just copy a wire and do nothing.

Part 2 · The factory tricks (no retraining)

No wasted steps: remove pass-through gates (connect readers straight to the source), and remove gates whose output is never used.

Parts in order: renumber the remaining gates so memory is read mostly forwards.

More per move: 256 images per block, so the CPU uses its widest instructions.

Faster counting: count the 1s for 64 images at once, instead of bit by bit.
