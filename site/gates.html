The idea

A computer's simplest unit is the bit (0 or 1). Bits are combined by logic gates:

GateOutput is 1 when…In plain words
ANDboth inputs are 1"only if both"
ORat least one is 1"either one"
XORexactly one is 1"one or the other, not both"
NOTthe input is 0"the opposite"

A normal network uses millions of gates just to imitate one decimal multiplication. What if the network were the gates?

What we built

Digits become bits. Each pixel answers 2 yes/no questions: "a bit dark?" and "very dark?". Each image = 1,568 bits.

A circuit of 32,000 gates in 4 layers. Each gate takes 2 bits from the layer before. The wiring is random and fixed.

Training picks each gate's type from the 16 possible 2-input gates. During training each spot is a "blend" of all 16, so it can be nudged gradually. At the end, each spot is locked to its best gate.

Counting gives the answer. The last layer is split into 10 groups, one per digit. The group with the most 1s wins.

Method from Petersen and colleagues, "Deep Differentiable Logic Gate Networks", NeurIPS 2022.

  • Gates beat light, because they have the if-then step built in.
  • After locking, the network runs on pure logic: no multiplication at all, same 92.8%.
  • Training mostly chose simple gates: pass a bit through, flip it, XOR, NOR, OR.
  • Size: about 125 KB, vs. ~460 KB for the standard network.
But…
  • Not a fair fight on accuracy: gates got 10 passes, the standard network 3.
  • On this laptop, gates answered 185× slower (3,400 vs. 628,000 images/s). Why that happened, and how we fixed it, is the next step.
Does it work for other data?

Yes, if the data can become yes/no questions: "age over 30?", "contains the word X?", "loud in this frequency band?". It suits simple decisions on tiny devices; it struggles with language and fine numbers.
