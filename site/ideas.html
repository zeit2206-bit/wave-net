Idea 1 · What a neural network is

A program that learns from examples instead of following rules someone wrote. It's made of many small "neurons": each multiplies its inputs by its own numbers (weights), adds them up, and passes the result on.

Training is a loop: guess → compare with the right answer → nudge every weight a little → repeat thousands of times.

Idea 2 · Where the knowledge lives

A trained network keeps no copy of its training data. It keeps only its weights. The knowledge is spread across all of them, in a precise arrangement:

"We compressed knowledge into a precise arrangement of numbers that only makes sense inside a specific structure."

Idea 3 · Is this the hard way?

A brain learns from a few examples on about 20 watts. Networks need huge data and energy. Maybe today's networks are like the old "circles on circles" model of the planets: they work, but there could be a simpler way nobody has seen yet.

Idea 4 · Waves and bits
  • Waves (light, sound) can compute on their own: when they meet, they add up or cancel. That's free math done by physics.
  • Bits (0/1, on/off) are a computer's simplest unit. A normal network uses millions of them just to imitate decimal math.

Both raised the same question: what if a network used the medium's natural operation instead of decimal math?

How we tested it

Every experiment used the same task: recognize handwritten digits 0–9 (the MNIST dataset, a classic test). Models learn from 20,000 or 60,000 examples and are scored on 10,000 digits they never saw. Everything ran on this laptop, using Python and NumPy.
